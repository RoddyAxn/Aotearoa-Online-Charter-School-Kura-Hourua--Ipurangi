PROPOSAL TO THE SPONSORING BOARD

Establishment of a National Online Charter School in New Zealand

Aotearoa Online Charter School | Kura Hourua ā-Ipurangi

Prepared for: Proposed Sponsoring Board
Date: September 2026
Status: Concept and Establishment Proposal

Initial model: Years 7–13 | Nationwide online delivery | 300 students | Growth pathway to 1,000 students

1. Executive Summary

This proposal recommends that the Sponsoring Board proceed with development of a New Zealand nationwide online charter school operating under the charter-school provisions of the Education and Training Act 2020.

The proposed school would initially provide education for approximately 300 students in Years 7–13, with a planned pathway toward approximately 1,000 students within five years, subject to approval, funding, educational outcomes and demand.

The school would operate principally through distance learning. Students would receive structured live teaching, individual learning plans, pastoral support, recognised secondary qualifications, teacher contact, attendance monitoring and access to practical and vocational learning.

The concept is legally viable. Section 212L of the Education and Training Act expressly contemplates charter schools providing distance learning. Where distance learning is offered, the charter-school contract must provide for how student engagement and attendance will be measured and ensured, together with pastoral care, online safety and adequate supervision arrangements for students under 16.

New Zealand already has an operating precedent: Aotearoa Infinite Academy opened in Term 1 of 2026 as a fully virtual charter secondary school serving Years 9–13.

The proposed school would emphasise live online teaching, practical New Zealand-focused education, NCEA pathways, financial literacy, digital technology, agriculture and horticulture, trades and vocational preparation, entrepreneurship, civics and law, and flexible education for students whose circumstances do not fit conventional classroom schooling.

Indicative first-year operating budget: approximately $4.75 million for an initial roll of 300 students.

The core educational programme should be sustainable primarily from Crown charter-school funding, rather than relying on donations or commercial income to employ teachers or maintain the core programme.

6. Legal Foundation

The principal legislation is the Education and Training Act 2020. The charter-school regime was inserted into that Act in 2024 and has subsequently been amended, including through the Education and Training (System Reform) Amendment Act 2026.

Under the legislation, a charter school is governed by a sponsor rather than the conventional state-school board model. The sponsor is a separate legal entity and may take forms such as a charitable trust, company, iwi organisation, education provider or other qualifying entity.

  • Governance and strategic direction
  • Employment of staff
  • School finances
  • School property
  • Policies and systems
  • Compliance with the charter-school contract

Every charter school operates under a formal agreement between its sponsor and the Crown. The contract establishes matters including the educational approach, target population, funding, attendance and achievement expectations, financial requirements, reporting and potential intervention.

7. Distance Learning Requirements

Section 212L of the Education and Training Act requires additional contractual provisions for charter schools offering distance learning.

Student engagement and attendance

  • Attendance at live lessons
  • Participation in tutorials
  • Completion of assigned learning
  • Teacher contact
  • Assessment activity
  • Project work
  • Participation in supervised educational activities

Pastoral care and online safety

The school would maintain dedicated online safety, cybersecurity, child protection and communications policies, with a named learning adviser for each student.

Supervision of students under 16

The enrolment process would establish appropriate supervision arrangements for every student under the age of 16, incorporated into enrolment agreements and school policies.

8. Existing New Zealand Precedent

A fully online charter-school model is no longer theoretical. The Charter School Agency identifies Aotearoa Infinite Academy as a nationwide fully virtual secondary charter school. It opened in Term 1 2026 and provides education for Years 9–13.

Its existence demonstrates that a principally online method of curriculum delivery is capable of approval within the present charter-school system. The proposed school would seek to differentiate itself through broader year levels and a stronger emphasis on practical, vocational and New Zealand-focused learning.

  • High-performance athletes
  • Young people working in arts or performance

  • Students working on farms or family enterprises
  • Students who have disengaged from conventional schooling
  • Students requiring more flexible learning hours
  • Students preferring a structured home-learning environment
  • Students requiring an individualised programme
  • Students wishing to accelerate particular subjects
  • Students pursuing vocational and practical learning alongside academic subjects

The school would not simply be “school at home”. It would remain a structured educational institution with teachers, timetables, assessment, attendance, pastoral care and educational accountability.

12. Individual Learning Plans

Every student would receive an individual learning plan identifying current achievement, curriculum level, strengths, educational needs, attendance expectations, qualification goals, career interests, vocational interests, support needs and the agreed learning programme. Progress would be reviewed regularly.

13. Attendance and Engagement

Online attendance requires a more sophisticated system than simply taking a classroom roll. The proposed system would combine live class attendance, learning-management-system activity, assignment completion, teacher interaction, assessment participation and verified practical or vocational activity. Students showing declining engagement would automatically enter an intervention pathway.

14. Student Pastoral Care

Every student would be allocated a named Learning Adviser. The Learning Adviser would meet students regularly, review attendance and achievement, communicate with parents or caregivers where appropriate, identify disengagement, coordinate support, refer wellbeing issues and monitor individual learning plans.

16. Teachers and Staffing

The school would seek to employ a predominantly certificated teaching workforce. A strong internal goal would be 90% or more appropriately certificated teachers, subject to any specialist positions appropriately filled under Limited Authorities to Teach or other lawful arrangements.

A realistic Year 1 target is approximately 31–33 FTE, rather than immediately establishing the larger 39-FTE aspirational structure originally considered.

Current Charter School Agency material identifies major funding categories including staffing, operations, property, services and distance-learning funding.

  • Operational funding

26. Funding Philosophy

The school should be designed so that Crown funding pays for the core school. Donations, grants and commercial income should support additional programmes rather than become essential to paying teachers.

  • Philanthropic grants
  • Charitable trusts
  • Corporate sponsorship consistent with school policy
  • Targeted education grants
  • Vocational partnerships
  • Research partnerships
  • International student revenue where lawfully approved
  • Community donations

None should be treated as guaranteed operating revenue without formal commitments.

28. Economies of Scale

A national digital school can achieve economies of scale because many costs do not rise proportionately with student numbers. A single LMS, accounting system, administration system, senior leadership team, website, cybersecurity platform, digital library and student-management system can support substantially more students without increasing cost at the same rate as enrolment.

29. Key Risks and Mitigations

Risk Description Mitigation
Funding risk Actual Crown funding could differ materially from planning assumptions. Obtain written funding calculations before contractual commitments.
Roll risk Fewer students may enrol than forecast. Use conservative staffing, staged recruitment and expressions of interest before opening.
Educational risk Some students may disengage from online learning. Use live teaching, active attendance monitoring, individual advisers and early intervention.
Online safety risk Students are exposed to cyber and safeguarding risks. Use school-controlled systems, filtering, monitoring, policies and trained staff.
Technology failure A major outage could interrupt the entire school. Use redundant services, cloud infrastructure, backup systems and business-continuity planning.
Regulatory risk Charter-school policy, funding and legislation could change. Use prudent contractual drafting and ongoing statutory monitoring.
Staffing risk Specialist teachers may be difficult to recruit. Use nationwide recruitment, which is itself an advantage of online delivery.

30. Strategic Advantages

Geography

Teachers and students can participate from throughout New Zealand.

Teacher recruitment

The school can recruit teachers nationally rather than within commuting distance of one campus.

Property

Far less physical infrastructure is required than for a conventional 1,000-student secondary school.

Specialist subjects

A specialist teacher can teach students located throughout the country.

Flexible education

Students can combine school with sport, work, farming, arts, family responsibilities or vocational activity.

Scale

Digital systems can support student growth without requiring a proportionate increase in physical premises.

36. Current Legal Position

The proposal has a sound statutory basis. The Education and Training Act expressly contemplates charter schools delivering education by distance. A charter-school contract for such a school must specifically address engagement, attendance, pastoral care, online safety and supervision for students under 16.

The Crown has also already approved and opened a fully virtual charter secondary school. Accordingly, the central question for the Board is no longer whether an online charter school can exist in New Zealand, but whether this sponsor can demonstrate capability, whether the educational model is sufficiently distinctive and effective, whether sustainable funding is available, whether student demand exists, whether robust pastoral and attendance systems can be demonstrated, and whether the Charter School Authorisation Board approves the proposal.

37. Proposed Board Resolution

39. Conclusion

New Zealand law now provides a practical pathway for a national online charter school. The proposed model is not simply an internet correspondence service. It would be a fully governed New Zealand school with live teachers, structured timetables, individual learning plans, pastoral care, attendance monitoring, online safety, recognised qualifications, practical and vocational learning, national access and contractual accountability.

A 300-student establishment roll creates a manageable first stage. A carefully controlled expansion toward approximately 1,000 students could create a substantial national education institution without requiring the physical infrastructure of a conventional school of equivalent size.

The next stage should therefore be formal development: establish the legal entity, obtain current Crown funding calculations, validate student demand and prepare the complete Charter School Authorisation Board application.
